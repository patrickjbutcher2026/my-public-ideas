# Daylight Engine
*A passive solar light harvesting and distribution pattern for civic and workshop architecture.*

## Purpose and Design Philosophy
The Daylight Engine treats sunlight as a mechanical resource rather than an aesthetic amenity. It is designed for settlements that:

- cannot rely on electric lighting  
- must minimize heat loss in winter  
- require predictable daylight for workshops and civic spaces  
- need infrastructure that survives decades of imperfect maintenance  

The philosophy emphasizes:

- optical efficiency without fragility  
- geometry over machinery  
- durability over precision  
- graceful degradation over peak performance  
- seasonal adaptability without moving parts  

The goal is not brilliance — it is usable, stable daylight delivered deep into a building.

## System Overview
The system follows a five‑stage optical‑thermal flow:

1. **Harvest** — capture sunlight through optimized apertures and collectors  
2. **Direct** — route light into interior spaces using reflective geometry  
3. **Condition** — diffuse, filter, or redirect light for comfort and utility  
4. **Store** — capture the thermal effects of sunlight, not photons  
5. **Release** — deliver stable illumination and moderated heat throughout the day  

A building becomes a light engine, not a passive container.

## Primary Components

### South‑Facing Glazing (Main Collector)
- Fixed glazing oriented for winter gain  
- Overhang sized to block high summer sun  
- Optional selective glazing to reduce IR load  

**Function:** Baseline daylight and winter heating.

### Exterior Light Shelves (Directional Harvesting and Steering)
- Mounted above eye level on the south façade  
- Upper surface bright white or reflective  

**Purpose:**  
- bounce sunlight upward  
- push illumination deeper into the room  
- reduce glare  

### Interior Reflective Ceiling (Diffuse Distribution)
- Matte or semi‑gloss white  
- Accepts light from shelves  
- Spreads illumination evenly  

**Function:** Converts directional beams into ambient light.

### Vertical Light Shaft (Deep Interior Illumination)
A robust, low‑tech light pipe designed for reliability.

**Geometry:**  
- square or rectangular  
- 45–60 cm width  
- height‑to‑width ratio ≤ 4:1  

**Top Collector:**  
- angled glazing cap facing south  
- optional fixed north reflector  
- no lenses  

**Lining Options:**  
- semi‑gloss white (recommended)  
- matte white (softest light)  
- reflective sheet (brightest but fragile)  

**Bottom Termination:**  
- frosted diffuser panel  
- optional spread box  

**Thermal Controls:**  
- vent at top  
- diffused connection to room  

### Thermal Mass (Passive Storage)
- Dark, high‑mass floor in sunlit zones  
- Optional phase‑change materials  

**Function:** Stores heat during the day and releases it at night.

## Seasonal Behavior

### Winter
- Low sun penetrates beneath shelves  
- Directly heats thermal mass  
- Light shaft receives stronger input  
- Building gains light and heat  

### Summer
- High sun hits shelves, not the floor  
- Light is bounced to the ceiling  
- Shaft receives diffuse skylight  
- Overhang blocks excess heat  

**Outcome:** Self‑regulating light and heat through geometry alone.

## Performance Characteristics

### Strengths
- Works under dirt, aging, and imperfect maintenance  
- No moving parts  
- Deep, even daylight  
- Reduces or eliminates daytime artificial lighting  
- Stabilizes indoor temperature  
- Survives long‑term degradation gracefully  

### Limitations
- Winter heat autonomy depends on climate  
- Cloudy periods reduce performance  
- Shaft must be sized carefully  
- Reflective surfaces require occasional cleaning  

## Recommended Use Cases
- Workshops requiring predictable daylight  
- Off‑grid or low‑energy homes  
- Resource‑limited settlements  
- Buildings with minimal maintenance capacity  
- Spaces needing psychological connection to daylight cycles  

## Future Extensions
Potential future standards include:

- passive heliostat arrays  
- luminescent solar concentrator (LSC) panels  
- fiber‑optic daylight routing for task lighting  
- hybrid thermal–optical façades  
- daylight‑driven projection systems
